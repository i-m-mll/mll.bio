Sometimes, a simple function doesn't seem good enough.

Here's one possible function we can draw, going from a set of players to a set of dice rolls.

![Dice function](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/dicefn.svg)

There are other, similar functions we could choose by changing where the arrows end... but any single function like this one can only give us a single outcome for each player.

As a model of dice rolling, this function is pretty useless! Each time a player rolls, hopefully there's actually a 1/6 (or about 17%) chance of each number turning up, not a 100% chance of just one of them.

How can we do better?

## Sampling different paths

Let's try writing our diagrams a little differently. Focus just on Max, for a second. He rolls five times: 5, 1, 2, 4, 2.

<Figure src="/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/samples.svg" alt="Samples">
Don't worry about the order of the rolls.
</Figure>

Let's merge all the arrows that arrive at the same place into single arrows. An arrow's thickness represents how frequently we follow it—so the arrow that arrives at 2 is thicker than the others.

![Distribution from samples](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/dist0.svg)

We can show the same thing using a *histogram*.

![Histogram](/series/probability-theory/assets/0-functions/hist0.svg)

Now divide his roll counts by 5—the number of times he rolled. This gives a *normalized* histogram in terms of the fraction of total rolls that turn up each way.

![Normalized histogram](/series/probability-theory/assets/0-functions/hist1.svg)

Now that we've normalized the counts, the sum of all the points in the histogram is 1, or 100%.

$$
20\% + 40\% + 0\% + 20\% + 20\% + 0\% = 100\%
$$

When we've got fractions that sum to one – or percentages that sum to 100% – across a set of outcomes, we have a *probability distribution*.

Our distribution says: given that Max is rolling the dice, there is a 100% chance that one of the six sides will turn up. According to what we've seen, 20% of the time Max arrives at 1, 40% of the time at 2, and so on.

Now split up the histogram bars, and arrange them vertically.

<Figure src="/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/dist.svg" alt="Distribution diagram">
This is redundant: the bars show the same thing as the thickness of the matching arrows. But we'll usually show both in our diagrams anyway. They complement each other, as we'll see shortly. Note that we also draw the paths-never-followed – grey and dashed.
</Figure>

Of course, five rolls isn't enough to get a good model of six-sided dice! We can't even roll once for every side. But if Max keeps rolling and rolling for a while, and we draw up all his rolls, hopefully we'll end up with something more uniform.

![Fair distribution](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/dist_fair.svg)

Whichever distribution we arrive at, at least we're now in a better position to model dice rolls when the outcome isn't fixed to one value or another.

<InfoCallout title="Perspectives on probability">

Two classic perspectives on probability are *frequentism* and *Bayesianism*.

It's rather frequentist of us to build a probability distribution for dice rolls by getting Max to roll the dice over and over while we count the results – and expect that eventually, our percentages will settle on the "true" distribution.

The Bayesian perspective is a bit more difficult to explain.

Bayesianism does not fixate on experiments that repeat as long as possible and eventually converge on the truth *independently of the strength of our prior beliefs*.

We always start with some knowledge. Often we have an idea of how likely an outcome of some process is, even when we've never sampled that process before.

All knowledge is connected. If we know enough about dice manufacturing and classical physics, we may be able to convincingly argue that the probability of rolling each number is about 1/6, without having rolled the dice even once. And even when we can't explicitly support our beliefs on knowledge from other areas of life, our rather huge brains provide us with intuitions that start to become useful well before we've made them explicit.

If I'm a Bayesian, I might hold a 1/6 prior belief *for any reason at all*. It might be hard for me to explain why I hold it, yet. But when my friend hands me dice and I roll 4 five times in a row – according to my prior belief, the chance of that is only about 0.01% – I'll have to start to *update* my belief to reflect that his dice are probably not fair.

From a Bayesian perspective, it isn't that repeated experiments (or significance tests) are useless – they're just one kind of tool we can use to update our beliefs. And they're limited by how they frame our questions: through a *repeatable* experiment with well-defined outcomes. But to be repeatable, the context in which it makes sense to perform the experiment must itself be predictable. Do we start from *predictable*, or do we arrive there?

Of course, we can still use frequentist tools. In practice, their limitations are manageable when we put enough effort into predicting (or maintaining) the contexts in which it makes sense to perform repeated experiments.

</InfoCallout>

## Channels

To properly model probability, we'll use *channels*. Here's an abstract example:

<Figure src="/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/chan.svg" alt="Channel">
We're not very likely to reach $\mathsf{y}_{1}$ from $\mathsf{x_1}$, or $\mathsf{y}_{2}$ from $\mathsf{x}_{2}$.
</Figure>

For each point in the source set, we get an *entire distribution* on the target, just like we did for Max's dice rolls.

<InfoCallout title="Arrow colors and distributions">

Whenever we draw a channel, any arrows that start at the same point will have the same color – and form a distribution. There's two source points in our last example, so there's two (vertical) histograms—one blue, one orange.

The bars of different colors grouped under each point in the target *don't* need to form a distribution. Here's a different channel between the same source and target sets:

![Another channel](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/chan2.svg)

There's an 85% or a 95% chance that we'll arrive at $y_3$ when we start from $x_{1}$ or $x_{2}$, respectively. So the bars under $y_3$ sum to 180%, and of course that doesn't form a distribution. But all three blue bars sum to 100%, as do all three orange.

</InfoCallout>

At the start of this series, I said we'd be dealing with functions. Now I've said that we'll be working with channels. But don't channels break the rules of functions? Multiple arrows start at the same place!

Well, when we want to get formal about it, *a channel really can be seen as a function*. It's just not as simple as the functions we've seen so far.

<NotationCallout title="What kind of function is a channel?">

A "simple" function looks like $f:X\to Y$, where $X$ and $Y$ are sets of outcomes. This won't work for probability, since for each $x\in X$, we can only reach a single outcome $y\in Y$ with 100% probability.

Now, say we also have a fancy function $\mathcal{D}(\cdot)$ that takes a set, and "expands" it to give us *the set of all possible distributions on that set*.

Then a channel from $X$ to $Y$ is a function $f:X\to \mathcal{D}(Y)$, which you might also see written $f: X\klarrow Y$. For each point in $X$, $f$ maps it to a single point in $\mathcal{D}(Y)$, like a function must – but that single point refers to a whole distribution over the points of $Y$.

Consider this channel from a two-element source to a two-element target:

![2x2 channel](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/chan_2x2.svg)

Here's how we could draw this channel as a function that respects the rules:

<Figure src="/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/chan_as_func.svg" alt="Channel as function">
Imagine how annoying it would be if all our diagrams looked like this.
</Figure>

Probabilities are real numbers. There are infinitely many real numbers. It doesn't matter that our target $Y$ has only two points in this case—we need to assign a real number to each of them, so there's an uncountably infinite number of distributions on our target. We've skipped over most of them in the diagram above, with the help of some dots.

There is one other way we could show this in a diagram. For each point (distribution) in $\mathcal{D}(Y)$ there are two real numbers (probabilities). They must add up to $1$, so we only need to remember one of them – if I know one is 0.6, the other one must of course be 0.4. So we could draw a diagram that maps to just one real number for each point in $X$ – say, the probability of arriving at $y_{1}$.

![Channel as function 2](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/chan_as_func_2.svg)

These two diagrams may be more formally correct than the multi-arrow diagrams we'll use, but I don't think they're as intuitive – and they'll quickly get ugly, when the source and target sets have more than two members.

</NotationCallout>

We'll keep breaking the rules, and draw our channels with multiple arrows starting from the same source point. While it's informal and "wrong", visually it'll be nice because it shows how a channel is a collection of *hypotheses* about how different outcomes lead to each other.

Suppose we're asking questions about a specific disease. There's a set $\mathsf{D}$ which has two points: does someone have the disease ($d\mathsf{+}$), or don't they ($d\mathsf{-}$)? And there's another set $\mathsf{T}$, also with two points: does that person test positive for the disease ($t+$), or not ($t-$)?

A channel that connects $\mathsf{D}$ to $\mathsf{T}$ describes how strongly we believe in the different ways this test can turn out.

![Disease test channel](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/disease/chan.svg)

If the person really has the disease, our channel here says the test will probably be positive—this is the large arrow labeled T pos., for *true positive*. And that's good, since we want the truth about whether they're infected!

But there's some small chance that the test will fail, and tell us falsely that someone with the disease is actually disease-free. That's the *false negative* case.

When someone *doesn't* actually have the disease, there are also the *true negative* and *false positive* arrows.

That diagram shows one example of a channel from $\mathsf{D}$ to $\mathsf{T}$, with specific probabilities. It describes a pretty good test, that usually gives accurate results. To describe a badly-designed test that usually gives a positive result, even when the disease is absent, we could write a different channel with a thick F pos. arrow.

And in a better world, maybe our tests would be perfectly accurate. That'd look like this:

![Deterministic channel](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/disease/determ.svg)

We're back to where we started: a channel with all its weight on one arrow per starting point is just a simple function!

![Function](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/disease/func.svg)

This isn't a useful model of the way disease tests actually work. Real tests aren't always reliable, just like (sadly) Max doesn't always roll ones.

<NotationCallout title="Conditional probability distributions">

The classic way of writing down a value from a conditional probability distribution uses the $p(\cdot)$ notation.

For example, if there's a 90% probability of T pos., we can write $p_{~\mathsf{T}|\mathsf{D}~}(t\mathsf{+}~|~d\mathsf{+})=0.9$, or simply $p(t\mathsf{+}~|~d\mathsf{+})=0.9$.

Here's another way we could write this, using functional notation we've already seen: $p(d\mathsf{+}\mapsto t\mathsf{+})=0.9$.

</NotationCallout>

## Channel composition

Say we have two channels, and the target of the first channel is the same as the source of the second channel.

![Two channels](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/comp0.svg)

These can be glued together to form a composite that – like a composite of simple functions – *forgets what happens in between*.

<Figure src="/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/comp.svg" alt="Channel composition">
On the right hand side, we go directly to $\mathsf{Z}$ without passing through $\mathsf{Y}$. For consistency, we'll keep the same colors for distributions reached from the same starting point: here the composite is a function $\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{Z}$, and it uses the same arrow colours as the first function $\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{Y}$, because both start from $\mathsf{X}$.
</Figure>

The weighted arrows make it easy to see which individual paths are followed often (like $x_2\mapsto y_1 \mapsto z_2$) and which aren't ($x_1\mapsto y_1 \mapsto z_1$).

To get the composite, we collapse all the paths that go from one point in the source, to one point in the target. For example, here are the paths that get collapsed to form the arrow $x_{1}\mapsto z_{1}$:

![Composition single arrow](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/comp_single.svg)

Even though there's a low chance of following the path $x_1\mapsto y_1 \mapsto z_1$, there's still a decent chance of following the composite arrow $x_{1}\mapsto z_{1}$ because we could also have reached $z_{1}$ through $y_{2}$ or $y_{3}$.

Paths get collapsed according to this rule: *multiply along, then sum across*.

**First, multiply along**: if there's a $10\%$ chance I'll reach $y_1$ from $x_1$, and then a $20\%$ chance I'll reach $z_{1}$ from $y_{1}$, then there's only a $10\%\times20\%=2\%$ chance I'll reach $z_{1}$ from $x_1$.

**Then sum across**: there are actually three paths from $x_1$ to $z_1$. After repeating the multiplication step for each path, we sum the results together.

<NotationCallout title="Channel composition">

If we have a channel $f:X\to \mathcal{D}(Y)$ and a channel $g:Y\to \mathcal{D}(Z)$, their composite is given by

$$
(g\ccomp f)(x)(z)=\sum_{y\in Y}f(x)(y)\cdot g(y)(z)
$$

A channel gives a distribution on (not just a point in) $Y$ for each point in $X$. So $f(x_{1})$ refers to an entire distribution, and $f(x_{1})(y_{1})$ picks out one probability from that distribution: the probability of reaching $y_1$ given that we started from $x_1$.

We use the notation $\ccomp$ instead of $\comp$ to make it clear that we're composing channels $f:X\to\mathcal{D}(Y)$ and not simple functions $f:X\to Y$.

Note that the equation is a sum of products. In our example above, if we label the channels $g$ and $f$, we can write this out as

$$
\begin{align}
(g\ccomp f)(x_{1})(z_{1})=&f(x_{1})(y_{1})\cdot g(y_{1})(z_{1})~+\\&f(x_{1})(y_{2})\cdot g(y_{2})(z_{1})~+\\&f(x_{1})(y_{3})\cdot g(y_{3})(z_{1})
\end{align}
$$

If you've studied linear algebra, this might look familiar: it's the inner (or "dot") product of two vectors.

Actually, we can represent our channels as matrices. Then the matrix for the composite channel is obtained by matrix multiplication

![Matrix multiplication](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/comp_matmul.svg)

And our calculation for the single arrow $x_{1}\mapsto z_{1}$ corresponds to the product of a single row with a single column.

![Matrix multiplication single](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/comp_matmul_single.svg)

Note that matrix multiplication uses the "last step appears first" ordering, similarly to $g\ccomp f$.

In more traditional notation, we could write:

$$
p(z~|~x)=\sum_{y\in Y}p(y~|~x)\cdot p(z~|~y)
$$

</NotationCallout>

<StringDiagramCallout title="String diagrams: Channel composition">

The string diagram for channel composition looks essentially identical to that for function composition.

![String diagram composition](/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/string-comp.svg)

When using string diagrams, because they are abstract, it's important to be clear about the type of objects we're working with. On the left hand side of this diagram, are $f$ and $g$ simple functions, or are they channels? In this series, by default the objects shown in our string diagrams will be channels, of which (as we've seen) simple functions are a special case.

</StringDiagramCallout>

## Remembering and returning?

Last time, we saw that under certain conditions, we can invert a function $\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{Y}$ to get a function $\mathsf{Y}\to\mathsf{X}$. Can we do the same with our channel?

It turns out the situation is not as straightforward as it was with simple functions.

If there's an $6\%$ chance I'll reach $y_1$ from $x_1$, and a $33\%$ chance I'll reach $y_{1}$ from $x_2$, what are the inverted probabilities I'll reach $x_1$ from $y_1$, and $x_{2}$ from $y_{1}$?

Before we can calculate that, we'll need a little more information. Specifically, we'll need the probability that we had started from each point in $\mathsf{X}$ in the first place. Our channel doesn't tell us that—it only tells us the probability that we *would* arrive at a certain point in $\mathsf{Y}$, *given* that we'd started from a certain point in $\mathsf{X}$. Consider: if we actually start from $x_1$ $100\%$ of the time, and never from $x_2$, does it make sense to say that we'd ever arrive back at $x_2$, once we reverse the arrows?

<Figure src="/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/chan_mask.svg" alt="Channel mask">
We can't just flip the arrows around. For one, $6\%+33\%$ doesn't form a distribution over $X$! We could force it to be a distribution by normalizing by the total, for example $\frac{6\%}{6\%+33\%}\approx15\%$. However, that would actually assume a 50:50 chance of starting from $x_1$ or $x_2$, which is not necessarily true!
</Figure>

Like with simple functions, we can run into the problem of unfollowed paths when trying to invert channels: when the probability of an arrow is zero, we never follow it – so we can't invert it.

Different from a simple function, a channel could reach *any* target point from *any* source point. And the only time we absolutely have *no* information about how to go backwards, is when *all* of the arrows arriving at a given target point have zero probability.

<Figure src="/series/probability-theory/assets/1-channels/chan_zeroarrows_comparison.svg" alt="Zero arrows comparison">
The channel on the right hand side is more problematic in general, because no matter where we start in $X$, we never arrive at $y_3$. However, if it turns out that we always start from $x_1$, then we won't be able to invert the channel on the left, either, because it specifically can't get to $y_3$ from $x_1$.
</Figure>

The solution to this problem is called *Bayes' theorem*, and we'll come back to it in a later post.

It will be extremely useful. We already have a channel that tells us how likely we are to test positive or negative for a disease, given that we have it or not. Bayes' theorem will let us invert it, to get a channel that tells us how likely we are to have the disease or not, given that we test positive or negative – which in practice is the information we usually need.

## "Conditional probability distribution"

The usual way that probability is taught, channels are called *conditional probability distributions*. Their functional nature is by no means neglected, but it does fade into the background much of the time.

On the other hand, it's very common for an experienced researcher to write $p(y~|~x)$ for a value in a conditional distribution, intuitively aware that they're talking about something function-like that starts from values of $\mathsf{X}$ and reaches distributions over $\mathsf{Y}$.

Here, we've kept functions in the foreground since the beginning. Taking this view, channels aren't just one more tool we'll use for probability. They are *the* atoms of the theory – its moving parts – and almost all of our tools will be special cases of channels and their composition.

Next, we'll see two of these special cases – and the fundamentally dual roles they serve.

<InfoCallout title="Bayesian networks">

Conditional distributions are also the central feature of the theory of *Bayesian networks*. Much of what we discuss in this series could be framed as a re-telling of that theory.

"Edge" rather than "channel". When we're discussing graph structures formed by multiple channels, it may be convenient to refer to those channels as edges. But in general, I think *channel* has a clearer flavour of the flow of causality or information.
